{% call m.drop('method', 'Method and limits', 'how each layer is built and what it cannot say') %}

Call. Tight, long or split is a judgment made from the three layers below, written on the date shown at the top. It is not a computed score, and it is there to be argued with.

Curve. The slope is read on the same interpolated curve as the arbs, between the day a cargo bought today loads and 90 days later. It is quoted a month, in price and as a % of the price at loading. Under 0.15 % a month a curve is called flat. Gas and gasoline curves carry the season, so their slope is flagged and left out of the headline.

Port flows. IMF PortWatch estimates the tonnes each ship class loads and discharges in a port every day, from AIS positions and draughts. Ports are grouped here by the trade they are known for. Dry bulk leaving Port Hedland is read as iron ore and dry bulk landing at Mundra as coal. It is an estimate, it runs about a week behind and it does not know the cargo. A ship with its transponder off is not seen at all, so Gulf and Russian loadings are marked as seen by AIS. Weeks end on Sunday and only complete weeks are used.

US weekly balance. The EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report comes out on Wednesday for the week to the previous Friday. The gas storage report follows on Thursday. Both are official and both are US only, which is why they sit next to world figures and do not replace them.

Dated facts. They are researched by hand. Each one carries its source, and a line without one is this page's own reasoning. The daily run does not rewrite them, so a line can be overtaken by events.

Limits. No balance sheet is built here. The page does not forecast supply and demand in barrels or tonnes. It lines up what a desk would check before trusting a forecast. Chinese and Indian domestic figures are quoted from the trade press. Exchange stocks are quoted as dated facts and are not refreshed.
