Which trades can pay more for ships

A long green line is a trade that can absorb a freight rally, which is demand support for that ship class. Red means the cargo only moves if freight falls. LNG is left out, because its break-even TCEs run into millions of dollars a day at these spreads. The axis is capped at 150 k$/day. Hover a dot for the real value.

Curves, or what time on the water costs

Each forward curve is shown as a % of its front month. A cargo is bought at the price of the day it loads and sold at the price of the day it lands. A backwardated curve is therefore a cost that grows with the voyage, and a contango is a gain. That is why steep backwardation shuts long-haul arbs and empties storage.

Method and limits

Chokepoints. IMF PortWatch counts AIS transits by vessel type every day. Weeks run Monday to Sunday and only complete weeks are shown. Ships with AIS switched off are not counted, which understates Hormuz traffic.

Break-even TCE. The daily hire at which the route's margin hits zero, given today's prices, bunkers, allowances and the curve. It is the most a charterer could pay for that ship and still move the cargo.

Bunkers and ETS. Rotterdam VLSFO for Atlantic legs and Singapore for legs starting east of Suez. Allowances use 3.114 t CO2 per tonne of fuel, or 2.75 for LNG boil-off, and 50% coverage for voyages into or out of the EU.

Floating storage. A VLCC at the model TCE, 7.33 bbl/t and 5% financing. It ignores the time to find a ship and the extra insurance in a war zone.

Limits. There are no vessel positions and no fixtures list. The page cannot say how many ships are open in a loading area, which is what moves spot rates day to day. The public build has no FFA curve.
